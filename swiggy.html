UX Research Study — Swiggy

Research Questions

Research Question 01

“How does the user interface of food delivery apps like Swiggy influence users’ food choices?”

Research Question 02

“What other factors influence users’ food choices on food delivery apps like Swiggy?”

Motivation

The topic was chosen taking into consideration the growing reliance on food delivery apps among individuals. As convenience becomes a primary driver, many users may be nudged towards unhealthy options through dark patterns (such as visual cues, placement, promotions, or algorithmic recommendations). This research is motivated by the concern that UI design may deliberately steer users away from healthier choices, shaping long-term eating habits in subtle but impactful ways. We chose the app "Swiggy" as it is one of the most widely used food delivery apps in India.

Data collection

12 Young adults (18 – 30) 7 Older adults (30 – 50)

Purposive sampling method — People who are well versed with using Swiggy.

Research Method

01

App Analysis

Analyzed Swiggy for 4–5 dark patterns influencing food choices.

02

Task + Interview

Scenario-based ordering task on Swiggy followed by semi-structured interview.

03

Screen Recordings

Users recorded ordering journeys; classified by dark pattern influence.

04

Eye Tracking

Gaze count on 4 healthy vs 4 unhealthy food options. 10 participants (18–30).

Data Analysis Method

PHASE 1 — Identifying dark patterns: Performed a Collaborative Walkthrough where we explored the "Swiggy" app and identified the 4 major dark patterns using predefined dark patterns taxonomy (Gray et al.)

Price framing

Using financial incentives to encourage less healthy but more profitable choices.

Examples

99 store, combo deals, discount prices

Obstruction

Making the path to healthier food more effortful or less obvious than default browsing.

Examples

Algorithm, default restaurant/food item line up

Deceptive promotion

Blurring the line between ads and personalized suggestions.

Examples

'Recommended' items, food ad banners

Aesthetic Manipulation

Unhealthy food has flashy images; healthy options look dull.

Examples

Differences in healthy vs. unhealthy product images

PHASE 2 — Identify the themes for both Research Questions: For Phase 2 Data Analysis, we performed Thematic Analysis of the data that we got through our semi-structured interview transcripts.

PHASE 3 — Screen Recording Analysis: For Phase 3 Data Analysis, we conducted a Screen Recording Analysis with Interviews of participants' ordering journeys, followed by Deductive Classification of dark patterns. Insights such as items ordered, dark patterns identified, and daily eating habits, etc. were compiled into a structured spreadsheet for cross-participant comparison and pattern synthesis.

PHASE 4 — Gaze Count Analysis: In Phase 4, we conducted Gaze Count Analysis. The goal was to identify whether unhealthy food items attracted more visual interest due to aesthetic manipulation.

Findings

RQ1: How does the user interface of food delivery apps like Swiggy influence users' food choices?

Visuals strongly impact what users end up ordering

Users are drawn in by food images, especially aesthetic, tempting, or familiar ones.

Add-ons at checkout push users to expand orders

Low-cost or well-placed add-ons during checkout lead users to make impulse additions.

Notifications have limited but strategic influence

Notifications rarely drive decisions but can nudge users sometimes or when creatively executed.

Familiarity and discovery are shaped by app experience

Users rely on recognizable names, previous orders, and app-driven suggestions to discover or stick to certain restaurants.

Offers, combos and limited-time deals detour from original intent

Visible discounts, combos, and offer timers often override users' initial food choices.

Q2: What factors influence users' food choices on food delivery apps like Swiggy?

Speed of delivery is consistently the top priority

Quick delivery is the most critical factor influencing food choice, often more than price or ratings.

Pricing, discount and cost related incentives drives most decisions

Price-sensitive users compare platforms; weak deals or extra fees reduce interest.

Cravings, impulses, and emotional connection to food drive spontaneous ordering

Familiar items and cravings, often mood-driven, can override planned choices.

Trust and familiarity with restaurants drive loyalty and limit exploration

Users stick to trusted places for hygiene and past positive experiences.

Food preferences follow a clear weekly rhythm

Weekdays favor healthy meals; weekends are for indulgent, preferred foods.

Meal timing, environment and weather shapes what gets ordered

Time, mood, company, and weather influence ordering and food choices.

Screen-recording + Interview Results (PHASE 3)

8/19 users' choices were influenced due to dark patterns.

2/7 of older adults got influenced by a dark pattern, whereas 6/12 younger adults got influenced by a dark pattern.

Obstruction Affected 1/19 users
Price Rating Affected 3/19 users
Aesthetic Manipulation Affected 4/19 users

9 out of 19 users ordered items that they don't eat on a usual/regular basis despite the given scenario.

We infer that due to mental association, whenever ordering online, users often gravitate towards unhealthy food choices.

Eye-tracking Analysis Results (PHASE 4)

Participant Gaze Data

Gaze count distribution per food option across 10 participants (Healthy vs. Unhealthy)

Higher visual attention often aligned with the final choice. 8/10 users chose items that were unhealthy. Unhealthy items appeared more visually engaging, indicating possible UI-driven influence on food decisions. Healthier items like Soya Mutter and Dal rice had occasional high views but were rarely selected.

Implications

01

Not all users are affected equally by dark patterns

Dark patterns affected only 8 of 19 users, with cravings or familiarity often being stronger influences.

02

The effect can be subtle

Dark patterns subtly nudge behavior, often boosting order value without being the main driver.

03

Mental association is a bigger driver

Users link online ordering with unhealthy food; dark patterns exploit this bias rather than create it.
