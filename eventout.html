Mitigating the Social Cost of Debt

About the Project

EvenOut explores the emotional side of debt between people. While digital payments have made transferring money effortless, asking someone to pay you back can still feel awkward. Through qualitative and quantitative research, we identified this emotional friction as the real barrier and not the transaction itself. EvenOut reimagines repayment as a neutral, lightweight interaction where the system takes on the uncomfortable part of asking.

Research

We conducted primary research to better understand our users through the following methods:

Demographics: 18-22 years

Qualitative interviews

We used open-ended, scenario-based questions to uncover the internal emotional narrative of lenders and debtors. This revealed how the "fear of pettiness" overrides financial logic and how specific relationship dynamics dictate repayment behavior.

Quantitative survey

We used Likert scales and numerical inputs to validate patterns across a broader demographic. This quantified the "Social Cost of Debt," measuring the specific financial "hassle thresholds" users absorb and the exact intensity of their social anxiety.

Sentence completion

We used projective fill-in-the-blank prompts to capture raw, instinctive reactions that standard questions miss. This identified the specific "tone" users desire in a solution and the core values, like relief and fairness, that drive their ideal experience.

Research Insights

The design process focused on understanding the emotional friction behind everyday lending and borrowing, and translating those insights into a more comfortable repayment experience.

Relationships shape repayment

The nature of the relationship influences how people lend, borrow, and ask for repayment more than the amount involved.

Social perception outweighs financial loss

Users are often more concerned about appearing petty or calculative than losing a small amount of money.

Debt carries emotional weight

For borrowers, guilt and the fear of affecting a friendship can make repayment feel more significant than the debt itself.

Awkwardness makes small debts easy to ignore

Users often let smaller amounts slide when asking for repayment feels more uncomfortable than the money is worth.

Ideation

To ensure our solution addressed the deep emotional friction identified in our research, we utilised a rapid Co-Design session with 9 users, combining two powerful methods:

Co- Design

Reverse Brainstorming

We flipped the problem by asking: "How might we make asking for money as awkward, judgmental, and friendship-damaging as possible?"

• Result: This exercise created intrigue and participants articulated the worst-case scenarios. We then flipped these "worst ideas" into positive design directions.

Crazy 8's

Participants were divided into two groups, given HMW statement for lender or borrower, to list eight solution concepts in eight minutes.

• Result: A consolidation of our previous discussion and some other out of the box ideas.

Ideation outcome

From the co-design activity, we consolidated the most relevant ideas into potential app features. Here are the concepts that best fit our use case:

Personalized Reminders

Customizable notifications with friendly, funny, or context-based tones, including memes and inside jokes.

Flexible Repayment Timeline

Allows users to schedule payments and adjust deadlines based on the comfort of both parties.

Gamified Experience

Customizable themes, interactive countdowns, and evolving profile characters that reflect repayment progress.

Adaptive Notifications

Reminder frequency increases as the repayment deadline approaches, keeping users informed without constant manual follow-ups.

Approach B: Relational Dignity (EvenOut WFD)

The Counter-Intuitive Bet: We hypothesized that in social finance, intentional friction is an act of care. We introduced three thoughtful steps: selecting a lighthearted vibe, adding contextual memory, and offering deadline flexibility.

The Breakthrough: Even though EvenOut took 15 extra seconds, 100% of borrowers settled immediately without awkwardness, because the interaction felt like two friends bantering, not a creditor collecting debt.
